Deterrence is the ability to dissuade an entity "from doing something by making them believe that the costs to them will exceed their expected benefit" (Nye, 2017). It's hard to achieve because its goal is to change or prevent behavior (Valeriano, 2018). Deterrence is a form of coercion that depends on credibility and the threat of retaliation; in cyberspace, efforts focus on changing behavior by targeting digital assets, information and networked systems (Valeriano, 2018).

Deterrence is possible in cyberspace, but very difficult, for two main reasons: **secrecy** and **attribution**.

## Secrecy

States want to keep their capabilities hidden. Under the security dilemma, no state wants adversaries to learn how to defend against, or build countermeasures to, its weapons. As Valeriano (2018) puts it, information is an equalizer: stealing intellectual property or military plans lets a rising power leap ahead technologically.

States without the money or time to build new weapons turn to stealing information instead. In the documentary *The Perfect Weapon*, CrowdStrike investigates a hacked client that builds satellites and stores the designs on its systems. Investigators linked the intrusion directly to a Chinese military officer. This is an example of a state trying to equalize: the stolen designs could help build satellites, or help find ways to attack them, such as the communication links between satellites and ground stations.

Secrecy also lets states keep weapons hidden until they're ready to use them. Stuxnet was the first offensive cyber weapon deployed by a nation-state to cause physical destruction (HBO, 2021). Iran's nuclear program was meant to be secret, but the attackers obtained specifications and built a weapon to slow it down, using multiple zero-day vulnerabilities (HBO, 2016). A zero-day is a vulnerability unknown to the vendor, so it can't be patched before attackers exploit it (Kaspersky, 2021). Holding several zero-days at once is rare, and the attackers saved them for this operation. Iran's secrecy failed, deterrence wasn't achieved, and the attack physically damaged its equipment.

## Attribution

Attribution is the ability to hold an entity accountable for its actions (Jordan, 2021). With VPNs and other obfuscation, finding where an attack originated can be nearly impossible. Yet credibility remains critical: holding true to commitments strengthens a state's ability to coerce (Valeriano, 2018).

There's a fine line here. Observers can infer a culprit from the political climate or a state's actions outside cyberspace, but hard evidence is elusive. States want credit for capability without being so overt that they invite retaliation. Prompt, high-quality attribution is difficult and costly, but not impossible (Nye, 2017). Investigations typically establish the entry point and attack type, but organizations often prioritize getting back online over deeper analysis. To deter future attacks, states will need to prioritize attribution and then response plans.

Deterrence through revelation can happen, if unintentionally. The Snowden leaks compromised NSA tradecraft but also signaled that the agency likely had more exploits than were exposed (Gartzke, 2017). That disclosure came from an insider threat, not a deliberate state choice.

## Cross-domain deterrence

Cross-domain deterrence (CDD) combines domains to deter a threat. The Pentagon recognizes five operational domains: land, sea, air, space and cyberspace (Schneider, 2019). Cyber operations are generally covert and hard to attribute, so they may not register in an adversary's calculations (Schneider, 2019). Attacks in the physical domains are easier to attribute, while a cyber attack may target information or availability without destroying property.

States are more likely to achieve policy goals by combining cyber with measures in another domain (Jordan, 2021). Because deterrence depends on credibility, and cyberspace makes credibility hard to establish, CDD is often the best option. But it risks a slippery slope toward escalation when deterrence was the only goal.

## U.S. policy

The United States has little established policy on using cyber for deterrence or retaliation. Schneider (2019) expects policy to stay ambiguous, invest in defense, and avoid CDD options that could escalate a crisis.

Open questions remain about when a cyber attack becomes an act of war: the first cyber attack, or the first physical retaliation? How the U.S. responds affects the credibility of future threats. There's also the question of attacks on companies versus states. If a company retaliated offensively, it could be seen as an act of war by its home country. Retaliating against the wrong state could start a spiral of escalation.

If states can't hold each other accountable, deterrence is unlikely to work, and adversaries will keep attacking without fear of response. The uncertainty created by cyber's secretive, virtual nature hinders its use for deterrence (Schneider, 2019), and cross-domain options raise the risk of unintended escalation.
