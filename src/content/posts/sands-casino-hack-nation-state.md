In February 2014, Iranian hackers attacked Las Vegas Sands to retaliate against its owner's public comments about Iran. Over several days they probed the network before finding a way in through a test web application at the Sands Bethlehem casino. It took several more days to obtain credentials that let them move from Bethlehem to the Las Vegas systems, where they destroyed data across the company's servers (Elgin & Riley, 2014).

These attackers were very determined, and it would have taken a lot for them to give up. Even if that first vulnerability had been fixed, I think they would have found another way in.

## Would I call law enforcement?

If I worked for a U.S. company hit like this, I hope I'd involve law enforcement. Customers' personal information and payment card data were compromised, which brings legal notification obligations to affected customers and regulators, and law enforcement can help with that side of the investigation.

But if I had good reason to believe the attacker was a nation-state, I wouldn't expect law enforcement to help the company recover or to hold the attackers accountable. Trying to hold another nation-state accountable for a cyber attack risks a political fight that could escalate into violence, or into a cyber conflict the U.S. isn't prepared for.

## Should companies hack back?

The article ends by noting that law enforcement wouldn't pursue the responsible state, and that there's debate about whether government or businesses should be able to fight back. With what I know now, I don't agree with giving companies that ability. Many can barely defend themselves, let alone go on offense.

## What companies can realistically do

I don't think an organization can fully defend against a nation-state. It can only prepare for an eventual attack by following best practices:

- Defense in depth
- Keeping technology and hardware up to date
- Following security policies
- Training employees on security

Beyond that, I don't think further preparation of the same kind is worthwhile. A company operating inside the attacking nation may get more help from local authorities, and may want to build a working relationship with that government to avoid being seen as a target by the country's hacktivist groups.
